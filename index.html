      4Optimal setup

      From your loads, location and target days of autonomy, three sized tiers with documented margins.

      Each tier lists usable battery energy (what the load can actually draw) and nameplate energy (usable ÷ DoD ÷ temperature factor). Apply uses nameplate only once. Minimum meets the worst-month energy balance and the target days exactly. Recommended multiplies energy (battery and array) and the inverter continuous floor by 1.25 — the same 125% figure used as a continuous-current multiplier in the NEC, offered here only as a planning margin, not a code requirement on kilowatt-hours. Comfortable uses 1.50, also a planning margin only. Inverter continuous is the maximum of simultaneous running watts, the largest single running item, and (daily AC Wh ÷ 24) × 1.25.

      How we calculate

      The formulas, and what is a fact versus a planning assumption.

      These results are estimates for planning only. They are not a design, a permit package or a substitute for the National Electrical Code, local code or manufacturer instructions. Use a licensed electrician for any grid or house tie-in, and size equipment from nameplates and the edition of the NEC your inspector enforces.

      • Daily watt-hours (AC): qty × running watts × hours/day × duty cycle. Duty is the fraction of those hours the device actually draws running watts. DOE / VCE: a refrigerator plugged in all day typically runs about one-third of the time, so duty = 1/3 and hours = 24.
      • Peak running watts: the sum of running watts of every item marked “Runs at the same time.” Presets default on for always-on or long-running items (fridge, freezer, CPAP, oxygen concentrators, routers and gateways, lights, HVAC, well and sump pumps, laptops and TVs) and off only for short-cycle items (microwave, kettle, coffee maker, dishwasher, range, washer, dryer, power tools, garage opener, phone charger, ham transmit). A warning appears if a load with four or more hours/day × duty is left unmarked.
      • Peak surge watts: for each item, the simultaneous running watts of the others plus that item's starting watts; then take the largest. Same idea as Honda's and Generac's “add running watts, then add the single largest extra starting amount.”
      • Battery energy: nameplate Wh = Vnom × Ah × batteries in series × parallels. Usable Wh = nameplate × depth of discharge × temperature factor. Series raises voltage; parallel raises amp-hours.
      • Sizer battery: usable Wh = (daily AC Wh / ηinverter + idle × 24) × days × margin. Nameplate Wh = usable ÷ (DoD × temperature factor). The two are shown separately. Apply sets integer amp-hours so usable is at least the tier’s usable target, and never divides by DoD twice. When Ah is treated as continuous, usable equals the target exactly.
      • Inverter continuous floor: the maximum of (1) peak running watts, (2) the largest single item’s running watts, and (3) (daily AC Wh ÷ 24) × 1.25. The 1.25 is a planning duty-peak factor — the same 125% figure used as a continuous-current multiplier, used here so the inverter can cover a modest concentration of the day’s energy above a flat average. It is not a code requirement. Recommended and Comfortable multiply that floor by 1.25 and 1.50. A rating check fails if the inverter’s continuous rating is below peak running, below the largest item, or if continuous watts × 24 is below the day’s AC watt-hours.
      • Runtime: hours = usable Wh ÷ (AC load / ηinverter + idle watts). The inverter idle draw is included as a DC-side constant.
      • Solar harvest (DC bus, Wh/day): array watts × PSH × derate × ηcontroller. PSH is monthly average daily irradiation in kWh/m²/day on a south-facing fixed flat-plate at the chosen tilt, from NREL/TP-463-5607 (1961–1990). Derate defaults to 0.86, NREL PVWatts' default 14% DC system losses (soiling, shading, mismatch, wiring, connections, LID, nameplate, availability). Inverter efficiency is applied separately when energy is used as AC.
      • Worst-month balance: harvest in the lowest-PSH month minus the DC energy needed for the day's AC load (load / η + idle × 24).
      • Open-circuit voltage in the cold: two methods. Manufacturer: Voccold = VocSTC × [1 + (β/100) × (Tcold − 25°C)], with β in %/°C from the module label. NEC Table 690.7(A): Voccold = VocSTC × Cv for the ambient-temperature band (UMD Extension EB-2023-0676 Table 3, excerpted from the 2023 NEC). String voltage = module Voccold × modules in series. Both must stay at or under the controller's maximum voltage.
      • Controller current: PWM uses Isc × parallel strings × 1.25 (NEC 690.8(A) style). MPPT charge current ≈ (array watts × ηcontroller) / battery voltage.
      • Inverter DC current: I = (PAC / η + idle) / Vbattery. A 125% factor is applied for continuous-current comparisons with BMS and wire (NEC 690.8 / 215.3 style).
      • Voltage drop: Vdrop = I × R × 2 × (one-way feet) / 1,000, with R in ohms per 1,000 ft from NEC Chapter 9 Table 8 (uncoated copper, stranded, 75 °C). Percent drop is that voltage divided by battery voltage. A 3% planning target is the NEC 210.19(A) informational note on branch-circuit drop — a note, not a mandatory limit.
      • Wire and fuse suggestion: pick the smallest 75 °C copper size in Table 310.16 whose ampacity is at least 125% of current and whose drop is at most 3%. Suggest the next standard fuse size in NEC 240.6(A) that is at least 125% of current and no larger than the wire's ampacity. This is a suggestion. Battery cable, 90 °C ratings, bundling and temperature correction can change the answer; a licensed electrician applies the adopted code.
      • C-rate: continuous DC current ÷ pack amp-hours. Compared with 1 C for LiFePO4 (e.g. a 100 Ah pack with a 100 A continuous BMS) and 0.2 C as a cautious lead-acid planning cap — edit if your manual differs. These caps are typical manufacturer practice, not a code table.
      • 12 / 24 / 48 V: the lowest of those three voltages at which 125% of continuous inverter DC current is within one 4/0 AWG 75 °C copper conductor (230 A, Table 310.16) and the voltage drop on your one-way run with 4/0 (0.0608 Ω/kft) is ≤ 3%. If none qualify, 48 V is still listed as the best of the three.
      • Quick-start systems: each chip loads its preset list, places the array in Chicago, IL (NREL station 94846, latitude tilt, December PSH 2.5 kWh/m²/day — a mid-continent winter stress case), and applies the Recommended tier: LiFePO4 at 80% DoD, 48 V (four 12 V units in series), 2 days of autonomy, default derate 0.86, ηinverter 0.93, idle 20 W, ηcontroller 0.98. Amp-hours are the smallest integer that meets the usable target. Array watts are rounded to 1 W; controller amps are rounded up to the next whole amp. Inverter continuous and surge are rounded up to the next 500 W, with a 1,000 W practical floor on continuous (a commercial house-use step, not a code rule). BMS continuous is set to at least 125% of inverter DC current. Module Voc is left blank, so the cold string-voltage check warns instead of failing — that is why a starter score may not be 100. 240 V loads (the mini-split, the well pump) switch the inverter to split-phase.
      • SOC chart: the pack starts full. Each hour, solar is added and the load is subtracted on the DC bus. Extra solar after the pack is full is curtailed; a shortfall shows as a deficit and SOC = 0. Daylight length uses Cooper's 1969 declination, δ = 23.45° × sin(360° × (284 + n) / 365), and day length 2 ωs / 15 with ωs = arccos(−tan φ tan δ), at mid-month, ignoring refraction. Irradiance is a half-sine between sunrise and sunset scaled so the day's integral equals that month's PSH. Always-on load energy is spread evenly over 24 hours; items not marked simultaneous are placed in a 17:00–21:00 window — a planning assumption, not a measured household profile.
      • Rating (0–100): start at 100. Each fail deducts 18, each warn deducts 8, never below 0. The checks and those deductions are listed on the Results page so the score is not a black box.
